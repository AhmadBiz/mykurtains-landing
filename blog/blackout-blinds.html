100% blackout: how to actually get total darkness.

Here’s the thing most people learn the hard way: a “blackout” blind on its own is not blackout. Light finds every gap. True darkness is a system — the right fabric plus the right fit. Here’s how we build it.

6 min readUpdated Aug 2026Montreal, QC

Why blackout blinds still leak light

Blackout fabric is fully opaque — no light passes through it. But an inside-mounted blind sits a few millimetres from the frame on each side, and light streams around those edges as bright bars. On a sunny morning that halo is enough to wake a light sleeper. To get real darkness you have to close the gaps.

The three parts of a true blackout

1. Blackout fabric

An opaque, coated or lined fabric. Available for roller blinds, honeycomb blinds, Roman shades and curtains (as a blackout lining).

2. Side channels

Slim tracks fitted to each side of the frame that the blind runs inside. They seal the edges completely — this is the single biggest upgrade from “dark” to “black.” Add a bottom seal for the last sliver.

3. Outside mount / overlap

Mounting the blind on the wall over the frame, larger than the opening, so the fabric overlaps the window on all sides. Simpler than channels; a little light can still creep at the edges, so we often combine an outside mount with heavy curtains.

Which blackout blind should you choose?

  • Blackout roller + side channels — the tightest seal and the cleanest look. Our go-to for nurseries and shift workers.
  • Blackout honeycomb + channels — darkness plus insulation and quiet. Best for cold bedrooms. See honeycomb blinds.
  • Blackout Roman shade — softer, more decorative; use an outside mount and expect a touch more light at the edges.
  • Blackout-lined curtains — beautiful and warm; overlap generously and add a wraparound track. Often layered over a blackout blind for a hotel-level result.

Honest pros and cons

Pros

  • Real, deep sleep — for babies, shift workers, light sleepers and anyone north of the 45th parallel in June.
  • Home cinema and screen rooms without glare.
  • Privacy is absolute when closed.
  • Blocks summer heat and, with honeycomb, keeps winter warmth in.
  • Works with motorization — wake gently on a schedule.

Cons

  • Fabric alone isn’t enough — you need channels or an overlap for true dark (we’ll spec it).
  • Side channels are visible — slim, but there; some prefer curtains layered over instead.
  • Costs a little more than a light-filtering blind of the same type.
  • Total darkness isn’t for every room — living areas usually want light-filtering.

Nursery note

For a baby’s room we recommend a blackout roller or honeycomb with side channels, cordless or motorized (no cords), in an easy-clean fabric. See our nursery guide.

When to choose something else

  • Living room or kitchen where you still want daylight? A dim-out roller or zebra blind.
  • Want warmth as much as darkness? Blackout honeycomb.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my blackout blind still let light in?

Because light leaks around the edges of an inside-mounted blind, not through the fabric. Fit side channels (and a bottom seal), or mount the blind outside the frame with generous overlap, or layer blackout curtains over it.

What is the best blackout blind?

For total darkness: a blackout roller or honeycomb blind running in side channels. Honeycomb adds insulation and quiet; roller is the slimmest and most economical.

Are blackout blinds good for summer heat?

Yes — they block solar gain very effectively. Light-coloured backings reflect even more heat.

Can blackout blinds be motorized?

Yes, and it’s a lovely combination — schedule them to open slowly at your wake time.
